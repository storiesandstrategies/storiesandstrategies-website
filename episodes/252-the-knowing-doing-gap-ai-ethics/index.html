The Knowing-Doing Gap: AI Ethics in Public Relations

Listen or watch: Apple Podcasts Spotify YouTube

There's a gap between knowing AI ethics and practicing them, and it's where reputations go to die. Research for PRSA's Board of Ethics and Professional Standards mapped it across 10 essential AI ethics competencies, from critical thinking and ethical judgment to privacy, accountability, and governance. The finding that stings most: critical thinking is both the most essential competency and one of the profession's biggest gaps. Communicators know the principles. They still fail to apply them when it counts.

Doug caught up with researcher Marlene Neill in Orlando at PRSA ICON 2026 to dig into what that gap looks like inside real organizations — the perfect storm of pressure for speed without the training or guardrails to match. They get into why communicators are weakest at judgment when judgment is the job, what value the practitioner still provides when AI can draft the release in seconds, and the one thing a solo practitioner or small team can do Monday morning to start closing it. If AI touches your comms work, this is the episode about the part no tool can do for you.

What Atul Gawande Teaches PR Pros About the Knowing-Doing Gap

The episode opens with a story about the most skilled professionals on earth — surgeons — and the embarrassingly simple thing that made them better. In 2008, a 19-item surgical safety checklist trialled across eight hospitals cut complications by more than a third and deaths by nearly half. Nothing about what surgeons knew had changed. Only whether they did it. Marlene's research tells the same story for communicators: training sessions happen, principles get recited, and behaviour barely moves.

Why Human Judgment Still Matters

Everybody is talking about how AI cannot have empathy or make ethical decisions, Marlene says, and that is exactly where the practitioner earns their keep. She recounts senior PR pros telling CEOs “we ought to go left here for ethical reasons” only to hear back, “that's not what ChatGPT says.” Ethical counsel has to be grounded in facts and case studies to have credibility — AI can codify ethics, but it can never determine them.

Verify Every Output

AI outputs sound convincing, and that is precisely the danger. Marlene shares the story of a practitioner who trained an agent in Claude to rewrite bios — it added a position she had never held. Another test produced an infographic with a culturally offensive image and spelling errors. The pressure to produce faster is real, but the communicator owns whatever goes out under their name, so pause and check.

How AI Is Changing PR Agency Billing

The conversation turns to money: the shift from hourly billing to value-based models. Clients won't pay agencies for what a junior staffer or an AI tool can do, Marlene's research for the Page Society found — they want strategic insight, thought leadership, and crisis counsel, the higher-level thinking the tools cannot accomplish. Mid-size agencies face the most churn, while smaller shops with specialized counselors are winning the strategic work.

How to Speak Up on Ethics at Work

About a third of US agency employees in Marlene's national sample said they faced retaliation or stayed silent when they saw ethical issues. She points to the “Giving Voice to Values” approach: don't just decide what is right — rehearse what you will actually say, anticipate the rationalizations others will use, bring an ally, and tie the concern to the organization's core values. Values that are aspirational rather than lived, she warns, destroy credibility with employees. The practitioner's credibility is their own — and silence is the one thing the research says is costing the profession most.
