A “Jolly” Rebranding Case Study

What happens when the name that helped build your reputation starts making your company look stuck in the past?

That was the challenge facing Greg Heilers and his team at Jolly SEO. After nearly a decade building a recognizable brand around earned links and search engine optimization, the company found itself operating in a search landscape that looked very different from the one in which it started.

Consumers were no longer relying exclusively on Google. They were increasingly turning to ChatGPT, Claude, AI-powered search tools, and other large language models to find information and recommendations. For Jolly SEO, the problem was not that SEO had suddenly stopped mattering. The problem was what the name communicated.

As Greg explains, “SEO was signaling to people that we weren't aware of AI search, that we weren't aware that a lot of consumers' behaviors shifted.”

The solution became Jolly Search.

Keeping the Equity, Changing the Signal

On the surface, replacing “SEO” with “Search” seems obvious. Getting there was considerably more complicated.

The team knew early that it wanted to preserve “Jolly,” the most distinctive and recognizable part of the existing brand. What had to change was the descriptor attached to it. “Search” was broad enough to encompass traditional Google search as well as the rapidly developing world of AI search.

There were practical considerations too. The team initially could not secure JollySearch.com, leading to months of discussions about other possible names. When the domain eventually became available at auction, the debate effectively ended.

But changing a name is only the beginning of a rebrand.

An established company has years of search authority, media mentions, backlinks, social profiles, and client recognition connected to its old identity. Redirecting an old website to a new one can temporarily affect search performance, and publishers may still have years of references to the former company name.

Greg acknowledges that disruption directly. A rebrand, he says, can create an “uphill battle” while search engines and audiences adjust.

The company is also working to create a consistent brand story across platforms including YouTube, Reddit, and LinkedIn. Greg believes that consistency is increasingly important because large language models are pulling signals from many different sources rather than relying only on traditional keyword-rich backlinks.

The Rebrand Wasn't for the Existing Customers

Perhaps the most interesting lesson came after the new identity launched.

Greg expected existing clients and contacts to have a noticeable reaction. Instead, most barely seemed to care.

That initially felt disappointing. Then a conversation with Claude helped him reframe the purpose of the entire exercise.

As Greg puts it, “The rebrand was not for the existing or the past 650 clients, it's for the next 650.”

Existing customers already knew the company. They trusted the team and cared primarily about whether Jolly continued delivering what it had promised. The people who really needed the new name were prospective customers encountering the business for the first time.

That is what makes the Jolly Search story a useful case study for any established organization considering a refresh.

A successful rebrand does not necessarily require throwing away everything that came before. Sometimes the better strategy is to preserve the part of the brand that already carries recognition and trust while changing the part that signals where the company is headed.

Jolly kept its recognizable identity but replaced a term increasingly associated with an earlier era of search.

The result is a brand designed not only to explain what the company has been, but also what it intends to become.

And ultimately, that may be one of the best reasons to rebrand: not to impress the customers who already know your story, but to make sure the next customer understands it.
