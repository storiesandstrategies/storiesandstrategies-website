What it Takes to Sell Your Brand Across the Atlantic

What works brilliantly in one country can fall completely flat in another. In episode 249 of Stories and Strategies, Doug Downs speaks with Daisy Pack, Managing Director of Hunter’s London office, about what brands need to understand before crossing the Atlantic—and why cultural relevance matters far more than simply emphasizing where a company comes from.

The conversation begins with one of advertising’s most famous examples: Volkswagen’s “Think Small” campaign. In 1959, the Beetle entered an American market obsessed with bigger cars, bigger engines, and bigger promises. Instead of disguising what made the car unusual, Volkswagen leaned directly into it. The campaign transformed difference into an advantage and demonstrated that being distinctive can be one of a brand’s greatest assets.

But Daisy makes an important distinction: standing out is not the same as connecting.

Difference Gets Attention, Culture Creates Connection

For brands entering a new market, Daisy says the real challenge is understanding which parts of their identity will actually matter to local consumers.

“Difference can earn you attention, but it’s cultural relevance that really helps turn that attention into connection.”

That lesson shaped Hunter’s work with Premier Protein in the UK. Although the brand was already successful in North America, simply repeating its American marketing strategy would not automatically make British consumers care.

Instead, Daisy’s team looked at changing social habits in Britain. Younger consumers were increasingly choosing wellness-focused activities, run clubs, yoga, and healthier social experiences over traditional nights at the pub.

That insight led to Premier Protein’s “Fueling Friendships Social Sauna,” launched around the London Marathon. The activation turned post-workout recovery from an individual fitness moment into a social experience centered on connection and community. All 150 tickets sold out, and the campaign helped generate a significant increase in product sales.

The lesson was not to hide Premier Protein’s American roots. It was to find a culturally relevant reason for the brand to belong in Britain.

Don’t Translate Your Brand. Translate Its Meaning

Doug and Daisy also explore the subtle differences between American and British communication styles. Americans may naturally gravitate toward enthusiasm, optimism, and bold messaging. British audiences, Daisy explains, often respond more strongly to understatement, wit, irony, and slightly offbeat storytelling.

That makes local knowledge essential.

“The key piece of advice I would give to any US brand is to have a Brit on the team.”

Without that perspective, brands can easily fall back on stereotypes—royalty, landmarks, exaggerated Britishness, or assumptions about what local consumers care about.

Daisy’s advice is ultimately simple: don’t make your brand less American just to fit in. Instead, identify which parts of your identity are meaningful to the audience you want to reach.

A brand succeeds across borders when it preserves what makes it distinctive while becoming relevant to the culture around it. That requires curiosity, local insight, and a willingness to listen before deciding what story to tell.
