The Hidden Rules of Workplace Emojis: What Gen Z Wants You to Know

Can a tiny emoji make or break the tone of a workplace message? In episode 251 of Stories and Strategies, Doug Downs speaks with Stacy Jo Dixon of Statista about the increasingly complicated role emojis play in professional communication. What seems like a harmless thumbs-up, smiley face, or heart can carry very different meanings depending on age, culture, context, and relationship.

The conversation begins with an important idea: text is easy to misread. Without facial expressions, vocal tone, or body language, people often overestimate how clearly sarcasm, humor, or warmth comes across in email. Emojis can help fill in that emotional gap, but they can also create a new layer of confusion.

What Gen Z Reads Between the Emojis

Stacy explains that Gen Z tends to be especially aware of how emoji meanings shift depending on the situation. A thumbs-up might look polite and efficient to one person, but to a younger colleague it can feel dated, dismissive, or low effort.

As Stacy puts it, “It’s also kind of the dad emoji, I think, to get this like thumbs up.”

That doesn’t mean the thumbs-up is universally wrong. Stacy says context is everything. The same emoji can be perfectly acceptable in one workplace and awkward in another. Culture matters too. She notes that some colleagues from the Middle East may see the thumbs-up as offensive, even though they understand it is used differently in Western workplaces.

That awareness is what Stacy calls “emoji literacy”—the ability to adapt your emoji choices to your audience, relationship, generation, and communication channel.

Some emojis, however, are much easier to rule out. Stacy advises steering clear of the eggplant, peach, poop, kiss mark, and sweat droplets emojis in professional settings because of their suggestive, immature, or ambiguous meanings.

Reading the Room Matters More Than Following Rules

The episode also explores whether emojis belong in communication with clients, executives, or senior leaders. Stacy’s advice is practical: follow their lead. If a client or executive begins using emojis, that can be a signal that a more casual tone is welcome. If they do not, restraint may be the better choice.

“I think with higher ups, maybe wait for them to use that type of communication and then that’s your green light to use that,” Stacy says.

Doug shares a useful cautionary tale of his own. He once used a smiley face to soften a blunt client email, only to have the recipient interpret it as condescending. The intention was friendly, but the impact was the opposite.

That example captures the central lesson of the episode. Emojis are not automatically professional or unprofessional. They are communication tools, and like any tool, they work best when used with awareness.

The smartest approach is not to use more emojis or fewer emojis. It is to use them deliberately. Know your audience, understand the context, and recognize that one tiny symbol can completely change how a message feels.
