The Platforms Under Your Podcast... They’re Changing the Rules

The first three stories this week have one thread running through them… platforms are quietly rewriting what counts. Whether that’s Spotify’s skip button, a YouTube view, or a podcast apps shrinking space in the ecosystem. The main takeaway for you from all this? You don’t control the distribution platforms… but you CAN control how you are exposed to them.

You Don’t Own Your Podcast’s Distribution. You Rent It

Spotify is testing a feature that lets Premium subscribers skip podcast ads with one tap. The industry reaction was predictable outrage, but the ad skipping itself isn’t the story.

Losh Moodaley lays out the real one in this piece. She argues podcasting is going through a quiet redistribution of power, where platforms increasingly control discovery, measurement, and monetization, and creators become dependent on infrastructure they don’t own. Her framework splits podcasts into three tiers: top shows with scale and leverage who barely notice, a middle tier that’s economically dependent on ad revenue but has no power to set the terms, and a bottom tier she calls hobbyists who don’t generate revenue so they don’t care.

Here’s where I’d push back a little, kindly, because it’s a smart piece. That bottom tier isn’t hobbyists. It’s most of podcasting. I mean the overwhelming majority of shows. For example, many are branded podcasts that were never trying to make money off ad inventory in the first place. Their value shows up as brand affinity, retention, or sales support, not CPMs. Losh’s exposed middle tier is real, but it’s a lot smaller than the framing suggests, and you probably aren’t in it.

What actually matters here isn’t the skip button. It’s what the skip button reveals. Spotify isn’t just a platform you publish to anymore. It’s a landlord, and every show that depends on it for discovery and monetization is a tenant living by the landlord’s incentives, not their own.

If your podcast’s business model depends entirely on one platform’s rules, that’s the exposure to worry about, not this feature specifically. Build the parts of your business Spotify can’t touch: direct sponsor relationships, an email list, IP you can license, a presence on more than one platform.

YouTube Clarifies What Counts as a View

Views and plays and impressions are getting a bit muddled at YouTube… but when you wrap your head around things it all makes sense. Coming soon:

An impression is if you see a thumbnail on your screen.

A view if you hover your mouse over that thumbnail and it starts to play a bit (you haven’t clicked yet).

And an engaged view is if it keeps playing “for some amount of seconds.”

The last part is admittedly a bit fuzzy, some amount of seconds. I’d always understood 30 seconds was used by YouTube but apparently not and has never been the case.

This matters for your analytics in YouTube Studio and particularly if you’re already monetizing through YouTube

Indie Podcast App Usage is Dying… Slowly

Independent podcast apps aren’t dying fast. They’re dying slowly, and Castro’s founder Dustin Bluck just published the most honest account of that decline I’ve read all year. The userbase for apps like Overcast, Pocket Casts, and Castro isn’t just stalled, it’s static and aging, made up of the same cohort that’s been listening since around 2015. All the growth in podcasting has happened on YouTube instead, and no app launched since 2020 has picked up any real traction, not even AI-forward ones.

What struck me most is his take on creator trust. He argues that removing ads or auto-generating transcripts and chapters risks breaking the “unwritten contract” between apps and creators, since disintermediated access to listeners is the only real thing an indie app offers over Spotify or YouTube. His advice for anyone still building in this space: embrace video now, invest in private feeds, and stop treating audio playback quality as an afterthought.

A Podcast Doesn’t Add to Your Content Workload. It Replaces It.

at Subwave makes an honest observation: a podcast in 2026 is not a single audio file. It’s the YouTube video, the social clips, the newsletter, the article pulled from the transcript, the show notes, and the webpage. The surrounding distribution work, he estimates, adds at least a full day on top of recording and editing and we know from experience he’s absolutely right.

Here’s the reframe worth sitting with. If you want to be seen as an authority in your space, you’re already expected to show up across multiple channels. LinkedIn posts, newsletters, YouTube, social clips, articles. The question is not whether that work needs to happen. It does. The question is where that content originates.

A podcast changes the equation. Record one conversation and everything else flows from it. The transcript becomes the article. The article becomes the newsletter. The clips come from moments that were already worth saying. Done right, a podcast does not add to your content workload. It reorganizes it.

The Real Estate Podcast… that Doesn’t Talk About Real Estate

True story… a little over a year ago we were approached by a real estate agent in Canada wanting to start a new podcast. His goal was to (steady for it) sell more real estate. In building the plan we recognized something about him… he’s extremely affable and community minded. So we pitched him… let’s do this podcast and NOT talk about real estate. Same goal, different approach. The idea was to join the journey your listeners are already on.
